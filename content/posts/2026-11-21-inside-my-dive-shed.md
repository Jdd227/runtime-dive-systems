I built a 12 × 16-foot dive shed to put my technical diving equipment in one dedicated workspace. It is more than gear storage. The shed has a gas filling station, a rebreather maintenance area, equipment rinse and drying facilities, a DPV rack, and a bench for 3D printing and electronics projects.

The interior is arranged around getting ready for dives, then cleaning, inspecting and putting everything away afterward. With only 192 square feet to work with, keeping the middle of the room open and making use of the walls and roof height has been important.

## The Workshop Layout

This is the current interior layout. The rolling JJ-CCR cart stays in the center when I am working on the unit. Gas filling and cylinder storage are on one side, with the rinse station, drysuits, tools and printer arranged around the perimeter.

![Current interior layout of the dive shed with rebreather cart, tools and fill station](assets/photos/dive-shed/img-4762.webp)

## Gas Blending and Cylinder Filling

The gas station is a big part of why I wanted a dedicated building. I dive a JJ-CCR and routinely manage oxygen, helium, nitrox, trimix, bailout and decompression cylinders.

The filling setup includes a wall-mounted USUN pneumatic gas booster, supply cylinders, regulators, gauges, high-pressure transfer lines and a Fortress 27-gallon, 200-psi compressor that provides drive air to the booster. The drive-air circuit is separate from the breathing-gas circuit.

The booster helps transfer and boost oxygen and helium from supply cylinders. Getting usable gas out of lower-pressure supply cylinders is especially valuable with helium. I keep the fill equipment and cylinder inventory grouped together so the connections, pressure readings and cylinders are accessible during filling.

I also use RunTime tools for gas calculations and cylinder management. It's an area I continue to refine as the equipment and workflow change. The fill station is not a substitute for gas analysis, oxygen-compatible components, clean handling or independent verification before diving.

## JJ-CCR Maintenance

The JJ-CCR is my primary dive system, so I wanted a work surface where I could assemble the unit, deal with the breathing loop, replace absorbent, inspect components, and prepare the oxygen, diluent and bailout connections.

I use a mobile work cart rather than fixing the rebreather in one location. I can roll it into the center of the room, work around the whole unit, and move it out of the way when I need the floor space. The nearby tool cabinet keeps ordinary hand tools and small parts organized without taking over the CCR work surface.

## Rinsing and Drying Equipment

After a saltwater dive in Puget Sound, the amount of gear that needs rinsing is substantial. A typical utility sink would be too small for a drysuit, wings, fins and the rest of the equipment.

I installed a large stainless steel dog-grooming tub as the rinse station. The basin is wide enough for bulky equipment and sits at a practical working height. Hot and cold water are supplied through PEX piping, with a compact electric water heater next to the tub.

Drysuits and undergarments hang overhead, using the roof height to keep them off the floor. The hanging area is arranged above the tub so dripping water can collect in the basin. Cleaning supplies and containers are kept next to the station.

## DPV and Fin Storage

Scooters take up a disproportionate amount of floor space. I mounted the DPVs horizontally on the wall using dedicated supports and put fin storage underneath. The rack makes the scooters easier to access while keeping the center aisle open for cylinders and the work cart.

Some of the mounting parts are custom-made, which is exactly the kind of small design problem my 3D printer is useful for.

## 3D Printing and Equipment Development

The shed also serves as the physical workspace for RunTime Dive Systems. My Creality K2 Plus printer has a 350 × 350 × 350 mm build volume, with a bench for tools, electronics and parts storage alongside it.

I use the space to prototype and test-fit equipment mounts, DPV accessories, JJ components, analyzer housings and other diving-related designs. Having the actual equipment next to the printer makes it easy to measure a part, print a revision and check the fit without moving between workspaces.

![Creality K2 Plus 3D printer on the product development bench](assets/photos/dive-shed/img-4761.webp)

## Power, Lighting and Heat

The electrical panel is rated at 100 amps. Circuits serve the shop lighting, heater, water heater, air compressor, 3D printer, battery charging and workbench outlets. The panel rating is not a claim about the feeder's available capacity.

The roof framing provides room for suspended LED shop lights and a ceiling-mounted electric heater. I wanted the work surfaces well lit and the space usable during Pacific Northwest winters, especially when I am handling wet gear or maintaining equipment.

## The Building, Briefly

The shed is a raised 12 × 16-foot wood-frame structure on concrete-block supports, with a 7/12-pitch gable roof, asphalt shingles, an exterior door and two windows. The interior framing remains exposed so I can move mounts, add outlets, or reconfigure storage as needed.

I coated the plywood floor with **waterproof deck paint**. It is a practical surface for the wet gear and regular cleaning the room sees, without putting in a separate finish floor.

## Still Changing

The current setup handles dive preparation, gas filling, equipment maintenance, cleaning, drying and fabrication in one place. There's still room for better cylinder organization and more equipment-specific storage, and I expect the layout to keep changing as I add and modify gear.

For a 192-square-foot shed, it does a lot of work. Zeus seems pretty comfortable with the arrangement, too.
