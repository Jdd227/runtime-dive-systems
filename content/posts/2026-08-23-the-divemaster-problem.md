Congratulations on earning your Divemaster certification. Now I have a question. “Are you a master of scuba diving?” I am not asking that sarcastically. I think it is an important question. Most newly certified Divemasters, especially the good ones, would probably answer no. They know they still have a lot to learn. And that is exactly my issue with the title.

This is not an attack on Divemasters. Becoming a Divemaster takes work. It involves leadership, rescue skills, dive planning, supervision, theory and professional responsibility. It is an accomplishment.

My issue is with one word: “Master.” Because words communicate something, particularly to people who do not understand the internal certification structure of scuba diving. And I believe the dive industry is using a word that implies considerably more than the qualification actually establishes.

## What Does “Master” Mean?

Outside of scuba diving, the word has a fairly obvious meaning. A master is someone who has achieved a high level of knowledge, skill and command of a subject. Mastery implies experience. Depth. Judgment. It suggests that someone has spent enough time practicing a craft to understand not only how things are supposed to work, but what happens when they do not.

“That is a high bar.” Yet in recreational scuba diving, we award the title Divemaster surprisingly early in what can be a lifelong development as a diver.

Inside the industry, we can explain that Divemaster is simply a professional certification level. It has a defined scope. It qualifies someone for particular duties. Fine. But that is not what the word says to everyone else.

## Two Divers on a Boat

This is the simplest way I can explain the problem. Imagine two divers sitting beside each other on a dive boat. One recently earned a Divemaster certification. They completed the course, met the standards and are excited about becoming a professional.

Sitting beside them is another diver. That person has twenty years underwater and thousands of dives across different environments. They may have extensive experience with cold water, current, poor visibility, wrecks, rescue situations, decompression or technical diving.

Now imagine that you are a new diver getting onto the boat. You know almost nothing about scuba diving. One person is introduced as: “Dave. He’s diving with us today.” The other is introduced as: “Mike. He’s our Divemaster.”

Who do you assume is the expert? The title has already answered the question for you. You have no way of knowing that Dave might have ten times the experience of Mike.

That is why I do not think this is merely an argument about semantics. The title signals expertise. And in an activity where people make decisions about whose judgment to trust underwater, signaling expertise matters.

## Certification Is Not Experience

Certification is important. Standards are important. Formal training is important. But none of them is a substitute for experience.

A certification tells me that someone completed a defined training program and demonstrated the required skills. It does not tell me how they respond when visibility disappears unexpectedly. It does not tell me whether they recognize a diver becoming stressed before that diver panics. It does not tell me what happens when several small problems begin stacking on top of one another. It does not tell me whether they have learned one of the most important skills in diving: Knowing when not to dive.

Those things come from time underwater. And even dive count tells only part of the story. A hundred dives repeating the same easy profile in warm, clear water are not necessarily equivalent to a hundred dives spread across different conditions, environments and progressively more demanding situations. Courses teach skills. Experience gives those skills context.

## I Skipped Divemaster

And then there is me. I intentionally skipped Divemaster. Not because I did not want more education. Actually, the opposite. I did not want the title because I did not believe I had mastered scuba diving. So I took a different path.

My diving progressed through a long series of stepping stones: Doubles. DPVs. Technical training on open circuit. Decompression diving. Eventually I moved into closed circuit rebreather diving.

That open circuit technical training did not become irrelevant when I moved onto a rebreather. It became part of the foundation I depend on if the rebreather fails and I have to bail out to open circuit. Then came more training, more complex planning, longer dives and deeper dives.

Today I have reached MOD 2 level CCR training. I have roughly 700 hours underwater. And I am nowhere near finished.

There are dives I want to make that I am not yet trained to make. There are skills I want to improve. There are instructors I want to learn from. There are areas of diving where other people’s knowledge absolutely dwarfs mine. I still have goals that require more education and more experience.

And after all of that, I would never introduce myself by saying: “I am a master of scuba diving.”

I am not. In fact, my experience has had almost the opposite effect. The farther I progress, the larger scuba diving becomes. Every new level reveals another discipline, another environment, another failure mode and another group of people who know things I do not. Scuba diving gets bigger as you learn more about it. That is why I find it so strange that the word master appears so early in the professional recreational diving ladder.

## Then the Divemaster Becomes the Instructor

This is where my concern goes beyond the title itself. Divemaster is usually not presented as the end of professional development. It is the beginning. The obvious next step is instructor.

Now consider what can happen. A diver progresses efficiently through recreational certifications. They become a Divemaster. Then they progress into instructor training. Now that instructor begins training new divers and eventually some of those divers become Divemasters themselves.

So potentially we have someone who has not mastered scuba diving becoming an instructor who helps create the next person carrying the title “Divemaster.” And that person is also introducing completely new divers to an activity where mistakes can have serious consequences. That deserves scrutiny.

## There Is Also a Business Behind This

Dive shops need customers. Customers become students. Successful shops get more students. And more students create a very practical problem: You need people to supervise and teach them. What does the shop need? Divemasters. Then instructors.

There is nothing sinister about that. A shop has to operate. Instructors need assistance. Classes need staff.

But there is an economic incentive built into the professional training ladder. Divers take more courses. Some become Divemasters. Divemasters become instructors. Instructors teach more divers. The cycle continues.

So I think it is reasonable to ask: Can the industry’s need for more professionals influence how quickly we create them? And if it can, where is the line between developing professionals and manufacturing them? At what point does it become a race to the bottom?

Because there is one thing no agency can compress into a course schedule: Time. You can accelerate certifications. You cannot accelerate experience at the same rate.

Judgment has to develop. Situational awareness has to develop. People have to see things go wrong. They have to make mistakes, learn from other people’s mistakes, encounter different environments and slowly build that internal library that tells them: Something isn’t right here. That library takes time underwater.

## What Would I Bring to a New Diver?

Suppose I decided to become an instructor. My technical certifications would not automatically make me a good one. Teaching is its own discipline. There are extremely experienced divers who are poor teachers, and there are less experienced divers who communicate exceptionally well. So yes, I would still need instructor training.

But assume two people have both learned how to teach. One went relatively quickly through the recreational professional ladder. The other arrived at instructor training with hundreds of hours underwater and a broad base of progressively more complex diving experience. Are we really going to pretend that experience adds no value to what they can give a new student?

If I taught an Open Water class, I would not be teaching students CCR decompression or bailout planning. But all of those experiences would affect how I teach the fundamentals.

I know why buoyancy matters beyond passing a skill. I understand task loading differently than I did when I was a newer diver. I understand why small problems need to be solved before they become multiple problems. I understand how stress changes decision-making. And I understand that being able to technically perform a skill is not the same thing as being comfortable and competent underwater. Advanced diving changes the way you look at basic diving. That perspective has value.

## Then My Niece Asked to Learn

This recently became personal for me. My 13-year-old niece wants to learn to dive. I would love to introduce her to scuba. But I cannot certify her. And I should not be able to.

I am not a recreational scuba instructor. I have not completed the training required to teach and evaluate an entry-level certification course. I agree with that system.

But now I have an interesting problem. To get my niece certified, I have to hand her over to an instructor. That instructor may be fantastic. But it is also entirely possible that the instructor has substantially less diving experience than I do.

Think about that distinction. My approximately 700 hours underwater and technical training do not qualify me to certify her because: Experience alone is not enough. Correct. But shouldn’t we be equally willing to say that an instructor credential alone does not tell us everything either?

When it is my 13-year-old niece, I want both. I want someone who knows how to teach. And I want someone with enough experience to understand what they are teaching beyond what appears in the instructor manual.

I want someone who recognizes stress before panic. Someone who values comfort and competence more than simply completing required skills. Someone who will slow a student down when necessary. Someone who understands that the objective is not to produce a certification card. The objective is to produce a diver. That distinction becomes much more important when the student is someone you love.

## And That Brings Me Back to Marketing

Why call the certification Divemaster? I think the answer is fairly obvious. Because Divemaster sounds great. It is aspirational. It sounds prestigious.

Look at the progression: Open Water. Advanced. Rescue. Divemaster. From a marketing standpoint, it works beautifully.

And recreational diving is an industry. Courses are products. Training agencies and dive shops are businesses. There is nothing inherently wrong with that. Good training costs money to deliver and good instructors deserve to be paid.

The problem begins when marketing language starts implying competence. Dive Leader would be accurate. Dive Guide would be accurate. Professional Dive Leader would be accurate. They just do not have quite the marketing punch of Divemaster. And I suspect that is precisely why the word survives.

## “Everybody Knows What Divemaster Means”

People inside the industry will say: “Nobody thinks Divemaster literally means someone has mastered all of diving.”

Maybe not inside the industry. But go back to those two people sitting on the boat. The new diver does not know. The parent whose child is taking a course does not know. The tourist diving on vacation does not know. They hear the word master, and the word already has a meaning.

If we have to explain that a professional title does not actually mean what its words appear to mean, maybe we should reconsider the title. Especially when that title carries perceived authority.

## This Is Not About Diminishing Divemasters

A good Divemaster should be proud of the certification. You completed professional leadership training. You learned to supervise divers. You developed rescue, planning and organizational skills. You became qualified to take on professional responsibilities. Those things deserve recognition.

So call them what they are: A Dive Leader. A Dive Professional. A Dive Guide.

None of those titles diminishes the accomplishment. If anything, I think they are more respectful because they describe what the person actually achieved without implying something they have not.

## A Request to the Training Agencies

So my request to the major scuba training agencies is simple. Take another look at the word Divemaster. Ask yourselves one question: Does this certification demonstrate mastery of scuba diving? If the answer is no, stop calling the person who earns it a master. Change the title.

And while you are examining the title, examine the professional pipeline behind it too. Ask whether meaningful experience should play a larger role before someone begins training the next generation of divers. Ask whether professional progression can happen too quickly. Ask whether the economic need for Divemasters and instructors is ever pulling in the opposite direction from the development of truly experienced dive professionals.

Qualification matters. Teaching ability matters. Experience matters. We should want all three.

After roughly 700 hours underwater, doubles, DPVs, open circuit technical diving, decompression training, CCR and MOD 2 training, I still have goals I have not achieved. I still need more training. I still have a great deal to learn.

I do not consider myself a master. I consider myself a diver who has progressed far enough to understand how much farther there is to go. And maybe that is the biggest problem with the word master. It suggests an arrival point. The longer I dive, the less convinced I am that there is one.
